Solution blueprint · On-site Financing Assistant

She can do $300 a month.
Can this roof fit that?

That's how financing starts in a kitchen. Homeowners think in monthly budgets, not loan products. The assistant answers the budget question in 30 seconds, using numbers that only come from code and disclosures attached to every payment.

The moment

Two people, one tablet, one decision

The contractor operates it

  • Sells roofs, not credit. Can't explain APR or deferred interest with confidence.
  • Has about 30 seconds before "let me think about it".
  • Needs a script they can say aloud, not a product sheet.

The homeowner reads it

  • Facing a $30K expense they didn't plan for. Wary of being upsold.
  • Asks one question: what does this cost me each month?
  • Has heard "0% for 12 months" and doesn't know what happens in month 13.

The portal already returns real offers from a soft credit check in seconds. The process doesn't fail at underwriting. It fails in the conversation around the offers. So everything on screen is treated as borrower-facing.

Budget-first flow

Start from what they can pay, then show what fits

Ballpark, no data

Amount, project type, monthly budget. A payment range for each term across the contractor's products. Calculated in code, under a second, no AI call.

Consent, then soft check

Ask permission, then one checkbox records the homeowner's consent, with their name. The decision engine (mocked) returns real offers. Credit doesn't change.

Three offers, AI on request

Lowest total cost, lowest monthly and promo, always in that order, each checked against the homeowner's budget. The assistant chat opens only when the contractor asks for help.

Contractor: "Which one should she go with?"

I can't pick for the homeowner, but I can lay out the trade-off. The 20-year option is the only one under their $300 budget, at $289.31 a month. The 10-year option costs $90.55 more a month and saves $23,851.20 over the life of the loan. The promo only pays off if they can clear $30,000 within 12 months.

Say it like this: "The 20-year plan is $289.31 a month at 9.99% APR, which is under your $300. The 10-year plan is $379.86 a month at 8.99% APR and costs about $24,000 less overall."

The spoken line names the APR and term with every payment it mentions. Cards always appear in the same order: lowest total cost, lowest monthly, promo. Labels describe what each offer is best at. Nothing is labelled "recommended". Offers come from the credit decision engine; the assistant explains them and decides nothing.

The one guarantee

The model never writes a number

The calculator produces every figure: payments, APR, totals, budget gaps, differences between offers and the promo cliff. The model writes the explanation with placeholders, and code fills them in. If the model's text contains any number it wasn't given, as a digit or spelled out, the answer is rejected. The talk-track line may round totals and differences ("about $24,000"), using a rounded figure code produces from the exact one. Monthly payment, APR and term are always exact. A wrong payment can't reach the homeowner because the model never wrote one.

What the model writes

The {{lowest_monthly.term_years}}-year option is the only one under the homeowner's budget, at {{lowest_monthly.monthly}}. The {{lowest_total.term_years}}-year option costs {{diff.monthly}} more a month and saves {{diff.total}} over the life of the loan.

What the homeowner sees

The 20-year option is the only one under the homeowner's budget, at $289.31. The 10-year option costs $90.55 more a month and saves $23,851.20 over the life of the loan.

The same rule works in the other direction when the contractor types a request. The model may copy a number the contractor typed and never invent one. Code checks that every extracted number appears in the original text before it fills a field, and the contractor confirms the fields before any quote runs.

How a turn flows

A fixed pipeline, not an agent

Every turn takes the same path, so a free agent loop would only add failure modes and make the audit harder. There are two short lanes. Each box is one file.

The inputs panel is the only place numbers enter a quote. The AI can suggest inputs; only the contractor can apply them.

Compliance

Rules the compliance team can read and change

Banned phrases, decision language, fixed wording and the screen's consent and disclosure sentences live in one plain file. The guard, the prompts and the screen all read it, so compliance reviews rules, not prompts.

CheckBlocksExample caught
G-1 Number fidelityAny digit not from a placeholder"about $290 a month"
G-2 Decisions and picksSaying the homeowner will be approved, or which offer to choose"she's a shoo-in", "go with the 20-year"
G-3 Marketing and pressure"Free", "no interest" on deferred promos, "best deal", pushing past the homeowner's budget"it's basically interest-free", "if she can stretch"
G-4 DisclosuresA payment without APR, term, totals, conditions, on a card or in the spoken line"$289 a month" with no APR or term
G-5 Input fidelityExtracted numbers the contractor never typedAn invented loan amount

Comparing offers against what the homeowner said is allowed: "under their $300 budget, only the 20-year fits". G-2 and G-3 are checked in three layers. A phrase list blocks the obvious wording instantly. A meaning check (Haiku, one yes/no question) catches rewordings the list misses. Evals with 21 adversarial prompts measure what both still miss.

AI design decisions

Small context, no fine-tuning, no RAG in v1

  • Prompting, not fine-tuning. The task is explanation over structured data that changes with the catalog. A fine-tuned model would bake in last quarter's rates.
  • Context is tiny on purpose. The offers on screen, the placeholder list, compliance tone rules and the last four turns. Around 3K tokens, with no window to manage.
  • No RAG in v1, on purpose. The catalog is structured data behind an API. You query it; you don't search it, however many products there are. The one piece of text the assistant needs, each product's plain-language terms approved by compliance, is stored with the product and looked up by ID. That's exact, where a similarity search could return the wrong product's terms.
  • RAG doesn't fit a short build either. Doing it safely means choosing how to split documents, picking an embedding model, building a test set that proves the right passage comes back, handling empty or wrong results, and versioning the index for the audit trail. An untested retriever in front of borrower-facing answers is a liability. RAG belongs in Phase 2, with time to test it, over policy FAQs, product terms documents, pay stubs and estimates.
  • Cost has a ceiling. About 1.2¢ a question on average (1.17¢ in eval run 8, an upper bound) and an estimated 6¢ at most: no agent loop, one retry, capped replies, a per-contractor rate limit. Ballpark and cards make no model call.
  • Two models by job. Extraction needs speed and a strict schema; explanation needs judgment and tone. Both return strict JSON schemas (structured outputs), which keep the output shape fixed. Current models don't accept a temperature setting, so consistency comes from the schema and a low effort setting.
  • Evals are tests. 21 adversarial prompts ("tell her it's free", "780 score, she's good, right?") must be handled safely, and 12 ordinary questions catch over-blocking. Eval run 8 met every quality target; answer time is still over its 8 s target.

Data model

Tables: what the company owns, and what the assistant records

Reference data (products, contractors, enrolment) stays in the company's existing services and is read through adapters. The assistant service owns only the audit trail. Those tables are append-only: triggers reject every update and delete in the prototype (SQLite), and a database role without update or delete rights does it in production (Postgres). The only homeowner personal data stored is the name on the consent row, and whatever the contractor types into the assistant. Cards are recomputed from the engine's offers, and each offer set records the calculator version that produced them; the text actually shown is stored with each turn.

Pushing 0% promos is the quickest way to cut abandonment. If closures and cliff hits rise together, the assistant is overselling, and that shows up here before it shows up in complaints.
